# Standards and audit workflow for native Android apps (30 October 2026)

Part of the Build with Accessibility for Android handoff pack. This is the reference the manual-audit module, the standard profiles and the report templates are built from: which standards an auditor cites in each jurisdiction, how every WCAG 2.2 A and AA criterion applies to a native Android app with the Android test procedure, what automation can and cannot judge, how audits are scoped and recorded, the Android developer guidance auditors check against, and which failures are most common in real apps. Every claim carries a source; unverified points are collected at the end.

## How the product uses this document

- **Section 1.10** (which standard is cited where) defines the app's standard profiles: WCAG 2.2 AA, WCAG 2.1 AA (ADA Title II), EN 301 549 V3.2.1 clause 11 (EAA and public sector; V4.1.1 once cited in the Official Journal), Section 508 (WCAG 2.0 AA plus Chapter 5), and the Indian IS 17802 and GIGW 3.0 references.
- **Section 2** (the criterion table) is the content of the manual checklist, one row per criterion with the Android test procedure, and the source of the Void and not-applicable rules per profile.
- **Section 3** tells the rule engine where to stop: which judgements are the human's, so the app never reports them as automated failures.
- **Section 4** fixes the finding record, the severity scale, the scoping model (screens, flows, sample) and the ACR and accessibility statement outputs.
- **Section 5** supplies the fix guidance (48 dp targets, 4.5:1 and 3:1, label rules, headings and panes, custom actions, live regions, Android 14 text scaling, Android 16 semantics and the deprecation of announceForAccessibility).
- **Section 6.6** sets the default rule ordering and severities.

## 1. Standards and laws that apply to native mobile apps (status as of October 2026)

### 1.1 W3C: Guidance on Applying WCAG 2.2 to Mobile Applications (WCAG2Mobile)

Status. The W3C Technical Report is a Group Draft Note dated 6 May 2025 (https://www.w3.org/TR/2025/DNOTE-wcag2mobile-22-20250506/; latest version https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag2mobile-22/), informative only, produced by the Mobile Accessibility Task Force (editors Jan Jaap de Groot, Jamie Herrera, Joe Humbert, Julian Kittelson-Aldred, Jon Gibbins) with the intent of publishing a Group Note like WCAG2ICT. The Editor's Draft (https://w3c.github.io/matf/) is dated 14 October 2026, and WAI still calls it "an in-progress draft of guidance for WCAG 2.2" (https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/mobile/). The May 2025 call for review is at https://www.w3.org/news/2025/draft-note-for-review-guidance-on-applying-wcag-2-2-to-mobile-applications-wcag2mobile/.

Scope and approach. It covers Level A and AA success criteria for native, cross-platform, hybrid and mobile web apps, mirrors the structure of WCAG2ICT (each criterion shows the WCAG text, the WCAG2ICT guidance, then the WCAG2Mobile guidance), and excludes closed functionality "where there is no assistive technology to communicate programmatic information" (https://w3c.github.io/matf/, sections 1.1 to 1.2).

Key interpretations (Editor's Draft, 14 October 2026, https://w3c.github.io/matf/). Web terms are replaced: the May 2025 TR used "screen(s)" or "view(s)" for "web page(s)"; the October 2026 draft uses "page(s)", defined as a distinct part of an app presenting specific content or functionality, and "set of pages" for pages sharing a purpose through structure and interaction (sections 2.5 and 2.7). "Application software" is the app and "platform software" the OS; native apps expose UI attributes to the platform accessibility layer directly, cross-platform apps usually through an accessibility bridge (section 2.1). Keyboard references also cover equivalent operation through the accessibility interface (section 2.4). Per-criterion edits (titles may be a navigation-bar title, top heading or active tab; mobile "links" include menu, navigation-bar and tab-bar items; pointer criteria apply to application software only with each layer owning its own pointer actions; 2.5.8 keeps the 24 CSS pixel floor but says touch "requires different and larger sizes, and for the sizes to be specified in native units as well as CSS pixels"; 1.4.12 is not applicable without a platform text-style override; 3.1.1 ties language to the app locale; 3.3.8 excludes platform passwords; 4.1.1 no longer applies; 4.1.3 must reach assistive technology through platform accessibility services) are carried into the table in section 2.

Still marked "Work in Progress" in the 14 October 2026 Editor's Draft (GitHub issue numbers in brackets where the draft gives them): 1.3.1 (#1), 1.3.3 (#24), 1.3.5 (#2), 1.4.3 (#28), 1.4.4 (#3), 1.4.10 (#4), 1.4.11 (#49), 1.4.13 (#6), 2.1.1 (#12), 2.1.2 (#30), 2.4.1, 2.4.3, 2.4.5, 2.4.7, 3.2.1 (#41), 3.2.3 (#54), 3.2.4 (#55), 3.3.2 (#45), 4.1.2 (#48). Because these are exactly the criteria that matter most for native apps (name/role/value, keyboard, contrast, resize, reflow), an auditing tool should fall back on WCAG2ICT and EN 301 549 clause 11 for them. [Partially verified: the list is as read in the live draft on 30 October 2026 in three page chunks; re-check the live document before quoting.]

### 1.2 W3C WCAG2ICT (Guidance on Applying WCAG 2 to Non-Web ICT)

Status. W3C Group Note; the current version is dated 11 December 2025 (https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag2ict-22/). Secondary sources date the first Group Note to October 2024 (https://abra.ai/blog/wcag2mobile-explained-what-the-new-w3c-draft-means-for-mobile-accessibility) [unverified]. It is informative and cannot be conformed to.

Key points for software (all from https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag2ict-22/). "Web page" is read as "software program" and "set of web pages" as "set of software programs", which "appear to be extremely rare", so 2.4.1, 2.4.5, 3.2.3, 3.2.4 and 3.2.6 bite only on such sets; the Note records that Section 508 and EN 301 549 exclude 2.4.1, 2.4.5, 3.2.3 and 3.2.4 for software. 2.4.2 becomes "Non-web Software Titled" (titles where the platform supports a Title property). 1.4.4 gets a software version tying 200 percent to the platform's text-resizing capability. 1.4.10 applies only where the platform can present 320 CSS pixels, otherwise evaluate at the nearest size. 4.1.2 replaces "user agents, including assistive technologies" with "assistive technologies and accessibility features of underlying software" and notes that standard components on accessibility-supported platforms usually already satisfy it. 4.1.1 is removed for WCAG 2.2. CSS pixel for non-web software: use the platform's density-independent unit (points on iOS and macOS, dp on Android, epx on Windows); "1 point means 1.333 CSS pixels"; absent a unit, take a viewing distance (about 28 inches for touchscreens) divided by 2688. Appendix A lists 34 criteria that are problematic for closed functionality (including 1.1.1, 1.3.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.4, 2.1.1, 2.4.7, 2.5.8, 3.3.8, 4.1.2, 4.1.3), relevant to kiosk-mode or locked-down Android deployments.

### 1.3 EN 301 549: V3.2.1 (2021-03) and V4.1.1 (2026-09)

Versions and legal status. V3.2.1 (March 2021) is the edition cited in the Official Journal under the Web Accessibility Directive and remains the legally cited version; V4.1.1 was adopted on 24 August 2026 and published on 2 September 2026 (https://nda.ie/news/en301549-published; https://www.dwt.com/insights/2026/09/european-accessibility-act-ict-standards-update). The ETSI PDF (https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/301500_301599/301549/04.01.01_60/en_301549v040101p.pdf) gives doa 30 November 2026, dop/e 31 May 2027 and dow 31 May 2028, and clause A.2.0 states that only "Once the present document is cited in the Official Journal of the European Union" does conformance confer a presumption of conformity with Directive 2019/882. No citation of V4.1.1 was found as of 30 October 2026; Deque expects it around 30 November 2026 (https://www.deque.com/blog/en-301-549-v4-1-1-is-final-what-changed-what-it-means-and-what-you-should-do/) [forecast].

What changed in V4.1.1 (foreword, ETSI PDF): Real-Time Text in clause 6.2 revised to include total conversation; "the requirements of clauses 9, 10 and 11 have all been updated to align with the WCAG 2.2 recommendation"; new Annex ZA (relationship to Directive 2016/2102), new Annex ZB (relationship to Directive 2019/882), and new clause A.2 enabling conformance evaluation of specific ICT products and services against the EAA. Clause 9.5 and C.9.5 list WCAG 2.2 AAA criteria informatively. The standard notes WCAG 2.2 is identical to ISO/IEC 40500:2025.

Clause 11 "Software" in V4.1.1 (ETSI PDF, pages 86 to 106). 11.0 names the covered software types, including "mobile applications", and NOTE 3 settles hybrid apps: a web view "is embedded in the app and thus does not meet the definition of web page", so clause 11 applies and "Where there is any doubt, clause 11 requirements have precedence"; NOTE 6 says software satisfies a criterion that does not evaluate to false (an app without pre-recorded media meets 11.1.2.2 automatically). Clauses 11.1 to 11.4 follow WCAG 2.2 numbering with Void placeholders. The non-obvious entries: 11.1.4.4 Resize text allows 200 percent "or, if the platform provides text resizing capabilities but it does not reach 200 percent for all text, up to the text sizing capabilities of the platform"; 11.2.1.1 Keyboard applies where the platform "provides a device-independent keyboard interface service" (an on-screen keyboard does not satisfy it); 11.2.4.1 Bypass blocks and 11.2.4.5 Multiple ways are Void for single programs; 11.2.4.2 Non-web software titled requires titles for "each window or screen" where the platform supports a Title attribute; 11.2.5.8 Target size keeps 24 by 24 CSS pixels with "on the same page" read as "in the same non-web software"; 11.3.1.2 Language of parts is Void because marking up all text "would be impossible"; 11.3.2.3 Consistent navigation and 11.3.2.6 Consistent help are Void; 11.3.2.4 Consistent identification is rescoped so that "components that have the same functionality within the non-web software program shall be identified consistently"; 11.4.1.1 Parsing is Void; 11.4.1.2 applies to functionality "not closed to programmatic access of information by assistive technologies". 11.5.2.1 to 11.5.2.17 set out the accessibility-services contract (object information, row and column headers, values, label relationships, parent-child relationships, text, list and execution of available actions, tracking and modification of focus and selection, change notification, modification of states, properties, values and text), which for Android is what AccessibilityNodeInfo must expose. 11.6 requires user control and no disruption of documented accessibility features. 11.7 User preferences requires software "not designed to be isolated from its platform" to follow platform accessibility settings, NOTE 6 listing "colour filters, contrast, text size, pointer size, and text cursor". Authoring tools moved from 11.8 to clause 5.10; there is no 11.8 in V4.1.1 (https://accessibilityteam.eu/guidance/en-301-549-v4-1-1-zmiany corroborates [secondary]).

Clause 5 generic requirements relevant to apps (ETSI PDF, pages 29 to 38): 5.1 Closed functionality (16 non-visual access sub-requirements such as speech output, masked entry, private access to personal data and receipts, plus text enlargement, keyboard-free operation and speech-free access) for kiosk or lockdown deployments; 5.2 Activation of accessibility features without "relying on a method that does not support that need"; 5.3 Biometrics, which "shall not rely on the use of a biological characteristic as the only means of user identification", so fingerprint or face login needs a PIN or password alternative; 5.4 Preservation of accessibility information during conversion; 5.5 to 5.9 (tactile operable parts, locking or toggle controls, key repeat, double-strike, simultaneous user actions) mostly hardware-facing; 5.10 Authoring tools.

CSS pixel definition. Clause 3 defines height in CSSpx with the reference-pixel angle 2*arctan(1/(28 x 192)) and the 1/2688 ratio, and NOTE 3 under 11.1.4.10 and 11.2.5.8 refers to the WCAG2ICT section on applying "CSS pixel" to non-web software (ETSI PDF). Reflow in 11.1.4.10 uses 320 CSS pixels wide for vertical scrolling content and 256 CSS pixels high for horizontal scrolling content.

Appt's analysis of V3.2.1 for apps: 44 WCAG 2.1 success criteria apply, six fewer than for websites, with thirteen wording adjustments (https://appt.org/en/guidelines/appt-em).

### 1.4 European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882)

Primary-text caveat: EUR-Lex (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng) and legislation.gov.uk's retained copy both returned bot-check pages in this session, so the directive's own wording is quoted from the ETSI V4.1.1 Annex ZB reproduction, and dates and exemptions from the Commission and secondary pages.

In-scope services (Article 2(2), reproduced in ETSI V4.1.1 Annex ZB.4 and applying to services "provided to consumers after 28 June 2025"): (a) electronic communications services except machine-to-machine transmission; (b) services providing access to audiovisual media services; (c) elements of air, bus, rail and waterborne passenger transport services: "(i) websites; (ii) mobile device-based services including mobile applications; (iii) electronic tickets and electronic ticketing services; (iv) delivery of transport service information, including real-time travel information ...; (v) interactive self-service terminals", with urban, suburban and regional transport limited to point (v); (d) consumer banking services; (e) e-books and dedicated software; (f) e-commerce services. The Commission's own page lists the covered products and services as "computers and operating systems", "ATMs, ticketing and check-in machines", "smartphones", "TV equipment related to digital television services", "telephony services and related equipment", "access to audio-visual media services", "services related to air, bus, rail and waterborne passenger transport", "banking services", "e-books", "e-commerce" (https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/policies/justice-and-fundamental-rights/disability/union-equality-strategy-rights-persons-disabilities-2021-2030/european-accessibility-act_en).

Annex I Section III, as reproduced in ETSI table ZB.4, requires making "websites, and mobile device-based services, including mobile applications, accessible in a consistent and adequate way by making them perceivable, operable, understandable and robust"; Section IV adds sector requirements for electronic communications, audiovisual access, passenger transport, consumer banking (identification, electronic signatures, information understandable at CEFR B2), e-books and e-commerce (ETSI Annex ZB.5) [sector detail partly from prior knowledge]. Dates and exemptions: transposition by 28 June 2022 and application from 28 June 2025 (Commission page above; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Accessibility_Act); microenterprises providing services (under 10 staff, at most EUR 2 million turnover) exempt under Article 4(5); disproportionate burden and fundamental alteration under Article 14; presumption of conformity for harmonised standards cited in the Official Journal under Article 15 (Wikipedia summary; ETSI A.2.0 confirms the mechanism); transitional period to 28 June 2030 under Article 32 [article numbers and the 2030 date not verified against primary text].

Practical citation for an app audit in the EU today: EN 301 549 V3.2.1 clause 11 (the only harmonised edition cited in the OJ, albeit under the Web Accessibility Directive), typically supplemented by WCAG 2.2 AA because V4.1.1 aligns with 2.2 and will become the EAA harmonised standard once cited (https://nda.ie/news/en301549-published; https://www.dwt.com/insights/2026/09/european-accessibility-act-ict-standards-update).

### 1.5 EU Web Accessibility Directive and UK Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations

EU public sector. Directive (EU) 2016/2102 covers public-sector websites and mobile apps; each needs an accessibility statement and feedback mechanism; EN 301 549 V3.2.1 is the harmonised standard; Implementing Decision 2018/1523 gives the model statement and 2018/1524 the monitoring methodology; Member States report every three years from 2021 (https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/web-accessibility). The 23 June 2021 deadline for mobile apps is confirmed via the UK transposition below (the Commission page gives only the 2016 and 2018 dates).

UK. The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018 (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/952/made) required mobile apps to comply by 23 June 2021 (https://www.gov.uk/guidance/accessibility-requirements-for-public-sector-websites-and-apps). GOV.UK guidance now states the requirement as the "WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility standard" (same page; guidance revised 5 October 2023), and GDS monitoring against WCAG 2.2 began in October 2024 (https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/category/accessibility-regulations; https://accessibility.education.gov.uk/training/wcag). Enforcement: GDS monitors on behalf of the Minister for the Cabinet Office and investigates complaints about public-sector apps; the EHRC enforces in England, Scotland and Wales and the ECNI in Northern Ireland (gov.uk guidance page above). GDS tests "both Android and iOS versions of an application" with the detailed-audit process, gives bodies 12 weeks to fix and refers results to EHRC or ECNI (https://www.gov.uk/guidance/accessibility-monitoring-how-we-test). Accessibility statements for apps must be published in the app store, on the website, or both, should be reachable from within the app, and "For mobile apps also include the application's version information and publication date" (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sample-accessibility-statement/sample-accessibility-statement-for-a-fictional-public-sector-website).

### 1.6 United States: Section 508 and the ADA Title II web and mobile app rule

Section 508 (Revised Standards, 36 CFR 1194 Appendix C, https://www.access-board.gov/ict/). E207.2 applies WCAG 2.0 Level A and AA to "user interface components and the content of platforms and applications", exempting non-web software from 2.4.1, 2.4.5, 3.2.3, 3.2.4 and Conformance Requirement 3; E207.2.1 reads "page" as "software"; E207.3 requires every program in a multi-step process to conform. Chapter 5 adds 502.2 (documented accessibility features: user control, no disruption), 502.3.1 to 502.3.14 (the accessibility-services contract mirrored by EN 11.5.2), 503.2 (honour platform colour, contrast, font type, font size and focus cursor settings), 503.4 (caption and audio-description controls at the same menu level as volume) and 504 (authoring tools). Because 508 uses WCAG 2.0, VPAT 508 editions carry no 2.1 or 2.2 rows.

ADA Title II rule (28 CFR Part 35, Subpart H, fact sheet https://www.ada.gov/resources/2024-03-08-web-rule/). Standard: WCAG 2.1 Level AA. Mobile apps are explicitly covered and defined as "software applications that are downloaded and designed to run on mobile devices" such as smartphones and tablets. Compliance dates were extended by the Interim Final Rule published 20 April 2026 (Federal Register 2026-07663): 26 April 2027 for state and local governments with a population of 50,000 or more; 26 April 2028 for those under 50,000 and for special district governments (ada.gov fact sheet; https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/doj-extends-title-ii-ada-web-2176958/). The substantive requirements are unchanged and Title III is untouched (https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/the-ada-title-ii-extension-what-it-doesnt-change/). Exceptions: archived web content, preexisting conventional electronic documents, third-party content, individualised password-protected documents, preexisting social media posts (ada.gov fact sheet). One source reports the extension is being challenged in federal court (https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ada-title-ii-web-accessibility-3432173/) [unverified].

### 1.7 India: RPWD Act 2016, IS 17802, GIGW 3.0, and the 2026 draft rules

- Rajive Raturi v Union of India (Supreme Court, 8 November 2024) struck down Rule 15(1) of the RPwD Rules 2017 as ultra vires for treating section 40 mandates as advisory and ordered mandatory rules within three months (https://www.barrierbreak.com/case-laws-that-are-shaping-digital-accessibility-in-india/; https://vinodkothari.com/2025/08/supreme-court-mandates-digital-accessibility-action-points-for-banks-and-nbfcs/); a 2025 digital-KYC ruling ordered all reporting entities to meet accessibility standards, appoint nodal officers and undergo periodic audits (https://www.disabilityrightsindia.com/2025/05/supreme-court-issues-directions-to-make.html).
- IS 17802 Part 1 (Requirements, December 2021) and Part 2 (Determination of Conformance, May 2022) are based on EN 301 549 V3.2.1 with RPwD-specific modifications; Amendment No. 2 to Part 1 is dated April 2025 (https://broadbandindiaforum.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/IS-17802_1_2021.pdf; https://services.bis.gov.in/php/BIS_2.0/bisconnect/knowyourstandards/Indian_standards/isdetails/MjY3NTI=; https://www.legalitysimplified.com/?p=63712) [BIS portal not read in full].
- Draft RPwD (Amendment) Rules 2026, S.O. 3962(E) of 16 July 2026, gazetted 20 July 2026 (Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment): scope includes "mobile and tablet applications", software, digital content and documents, including establishments abroad serving persons in India; mandates IS 17802 Parts 1 and 2; requires a signed, machine-readable Accessibility Conformance Report prepared or verified by accessibility-testing experts; one year for establishments with turnover of INR 500 crore or more, 18 months for smaller ones, two years for full compliance under section 46; penalties under section 89 with 90-day remediation orders. Still a draft at the 29 July 2026 article date (https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/compliance/the-new-accessibility-conformance-regime-key-takeaways-from-the-draft-rpwd-%28amendment%29-rules-2026); no final notification found by 30 October 2026 [unverified].
- GIGW 3.0 "ensures conformity with Level AA of WCAG 2.1", adds 17 success criteria including for "disabilities on mobile devices", covers "websites, web portals, web applications and mobile apps", and is certified through STQC (https://guidelines.india.gov.in/?p=10063); vendor sources cite 88 mandatory guidelines, about 50 on accessibility (https://www.impriindia.com/insights/digital-governance-gigw-3-0/) [unverified].

### 1.8 Canada

Amendments to the Accessible Canada Regulations (Canada Gazette Part II, 17 December 2025) require federally regulated organisations to follow "the most recent version of CAN/ASC-EN 301 549 set by Accessibility Standards Canada". Dates: new and updated web pages 5 December 2027 (federal public sector) and 5 December 2028 (large and medium businesses); new public mobile applications, assessment of existing public mobile applications and digital documents 5 December 2028 (federal public sector and large businesses); accessibility statements for federal websites by 5 December 2027, adding apps and documents by 5 December 2028; procurement checks for web and app purchases from 5 December 2028; exemptions for historical items, user-generated content and employee teamwork platforms; maps and technical drawings "as much as possible" (https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/accessible-canada/regulations-summary-act/amendment.html). CAN/ASC-EN 301 549:2024 is an identical adoption of EN 301 549:2021, that is V3.2.1 (https://accessible.canada.ca/standards-and-technical-guides/standards-and-technical-guides-database/can-asc-en-301-5492024-accessibility-requirements-ict-products-and-services-en-301-5492021-idt).

### 1.9 Australia

The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 is the legal hook (https://www.w3.org/WAI/policies/australia/). ## 2. WCAG 2.2 Level A and AA applied to native Android

Legend. "M" is WCAG2Mobile Editor's Draft, 14 October 2026 (https://w3c.github.io/matf/). "I" is WCAG2ICT Group Note, 11 December 2025 (https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag2ict-22/). "EN" is the EN 301 549 V4.1.1 clause (ETSI PDF). Android test procedures draw on https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/testing, https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/6376559 (Accessibility Scanner), https://a11y-guidelines.orange.com/en/mobile/android/test/manual-testing/, https://github.com/alphagov/wcag-primer/wiki/mobile-app-testing, https://appt.org/en/guidelines/appt-em and the GDS monitoring report. "Scanner" means Accessibility Scanner (Accessibility Test Framework checks).

## 3. What can be automated on device and what needs a human

### 3.1 Published coverage estimates

- Deque's Automated Accessibility Coverage Report: 57.38 percent of issue instances across more than 2,000 first-time audits and 13,000 pages were found by axe-core automation; automated findings touched 16 of 50 WCAG 2.1 A and AA criteria; 1.4.3 alone was 30.08 percent of all issues and 83.11 percent of those were found automatically. The report states "This study focused on HTML pages only", so it is a web figure (https://www.deque.com/automated-accessibility-coverage-report/). Deque's separate semi-automated report claims "80+% of accessibility issues by volume" with Intelligent Guided Tests, again for web (same page).
- ScreenAudit (CHI 2025, University of Washington): across 14 frequently downloaded Android app screens reviewed by six accessibility experts, the Accessibility Scanner covered only 31.3 percent of the screen-reader errors in the expert ground truth, against 69.2 percent (precision 71.3 percent) for the LLM-based ScreenAudit; the paper also cites Carvalho et al., who found 40 distinct accessibility problem types for blind and low-vision users across eight apps and sites of which automated checkers detected four (https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-25.02.pdf). Its ground-truth taxonomy of 306 element judgements: Missing Label 39 (1.1.1), Label Quality 42 (2.4.4, 2.4.6, 4.1.2), Structure and Grouping 54 (1.3.1, 1.3.2, 2.4.3, 3.2.3), Heading 16 (2.4.2, 2.4.10), Functionality 12 (2.1.1, 3.2.1, 3.2.2, 4.1.3), no error 143.
- Google's own documentation says the Accessibility Test Framework "can't detect issues that occur only while the app runs on a device" and frames Scanner and the Play pre-launch report as covering content labels, touch targets, contrast and some implementation attributes, with TalkBack, Switch Access and user testing required for everything else (https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/testing).
- Xbot's exploration of 2,270 apps used the Accessibility Test Framework's 10 issue types; five of them (item label, item descriptions, touch target, text contrast, image contrast) were 93.1 percent of findings, which says as much about what automation can see as about prevalence (https://arxiv.org/html/2203.06422v1).
- Evinced publishes no measured mobile coverage figure; a single third-party run on one Android app had the Evinced SDK report 89 issues (40 violations, 49 needing human review) while ATF found 10 problems Evinced missed (https://axelerant.com/blog/native-vs.-commercial-automated-solutions-for-accessibility-testing) [not generalisable]. Vendor "25 to 40 percent" mobile figures give no methodology (https://testparty.ai/blog/mobile-accessibility-testing-remediation-guide) [unverified].
- BBC's bbc-a11y coverage document marks only 12 of roughly 50 Mobile Accessibility Guidelines as automatable (content resizing, visible focus, indicating language, focusable elements, labelling form controls, managing focus, headings, containers and landmarks, alternatives for non-text content, tooltips, visual formatting, with unique screen titles semi-automated); everything in audio and video, colour, touch target size, spacing, actionable elements, consistency, focus order, keyboard trap, images of text, links, notifications, timeouts and roles is manual (https://github.com/bbc/bbc-a11y/blob/HEAD/guides/coverage.md).

### 3.2 What on-device automation checks today

Accessibility Test Framework for Android, the engine behind Accessibility Scanner, Espresso accessibility checks, the Play pre-launch report and Compose UI Check, ships 14 check classes: ClassNameCheck, ClickableSpanCheck, DuplicateClickableBoundsCheck, DuplicateSpeakableTextCheck, EditableContentDescCheck, ImageContrastCheck, LinkPurposeUnclearCheck, RedundantDescriptionCheck, SpeakableTextPresentCheck, TextContrastCheck, TextSizeCheck, TouchTargetSizeCheck, TraversalOrderCheck, UnexposedTextCheck (https://github.com/google/Accessibility-Test-Framework-for-Android/tree/master/src/main/java/com/google/android/apps/common/testing/accessibility/framework/checks). Scanner's help page groups them as content labelling (missing label, label includes type or state, duplicate descriptions, unclear link purpose, unexposed text), implementation (ClickableSpan, duplicate clickable elements, editable item has contentDescription, unsupported item type, traversal order, text scaling), touch target size (under 48 by 48 dp, 32 dp for IME windows or display edge, adjustable) and low contrast (text and image below 3.0 by default, adjustable) (https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/6376559). The Play pre-launch report groups into touch target size, low contrast, content labelling and implementation (https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/testing). axe DevTools Mobile adds rules such as Color Contrast (computer-vision based, only on rendered views), Label In Name (2.5.3), Label At Front, Touch Target Size (mapped to 2.5.5 AAA), Touch Target Spacing (2.5.8), Hidden Active View Focus (2.4.3) and Hidden Informative View Focus (1.3.2), with separate XML and Compose rule sets (https://docs.deque.com/devtools-mobile/2024.2.14/en/android-rules; https://docs.deque.com/devtools-mobile/2023.4.19/en/android-rules-xml).

What automation on Android therefore does not judge: whether a label is meaningful rather than merely present; role and state correctness of custom controls; focus order and focus management across screen changes; keyboard operability and traps; gesture alternatives; reflow and layout survival at 200 percent and large display size; orientation; status-message announcements; timeouts; error identification semantics; media alternatives; consistency across screens. Both Google's guidance and ScreenAudit's expert review say these require TalkBack, Switch Access and keyboard testing by a person.

### 3.3 Manual tests every mobile methodology includes

Union of the Android developer testing guide (https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/testing), GDS monitoring (report URL above), Orange (https://a11y-guidelines.orange.com/en/mobile/android/test/manual-testing/), the alphagov wiki (https://github.com/alphagov/wcag-primer/wiki/mobile-app-testing, edited 4 December 2024), Appt-EM (https://appt.org/en/guidelines/appt-em), Accessible.org (https://accessible.org/conduct-audit-native-mobile-app/), the ArcTouch and Fable method (SOMAA PDF) and the TPG Mobile Testing Guide version 3, August 2019 (https://www.tpgi.com/downloads/TPG_Mobile_Testing_Guide.pdf); BBC MAG categories come from a mirror and the bbc-a11y coverage file:

1. TalkBack full traversal of every screen: every element reachable by swipe; announcements convey purpose, role, state and value succinctly; alerts and temporary messages read aloud; main workflows completable; decorative images skipped; off-screen "ghost" items not read; dynamic content, activity titles and horizontal scrolls announced (Google, Orange, GDS, Appt-EM minimum "screen reader").
2. Switch Access: whole workflow completable; items highlighted only if actionable and only once; gesture-only functions available as controls or custom actions; group selection shows all and only actionable items (Google, Orange, Accessible.org).
3. Voice Access: no two interactive elements share a label on one screen; app opens by name; all actions by command (Google, Orange, ArcTouch "alternative navigation").
4. External Bluetooth keyboard: Tab, Shift+Tab, arrows, Enter, Space reach and operate everything; focus always visible; no traps; exiting a feature impossible counts as blocking (GDS, Orange, alphagov).
5. Font size at maximum (200 percent) and Display size at maximum: no clipping, overlap or lost controls (Google Android 14 guidance, Orange, Appt-EM minimum "larger text", ArcTouch "font scaling").
6. Magnification: zoomed content remains readable and operable (Orange, Accessible.org, TPG).
7. Orientation: portrait and landscape on every screen (Orange, ArcTouch, GDS found this least likely to be fixed).
8. Time to take action: UI changes that depend on delays respect the accessibility timeout (Orange).
9. Colour and contrast: CCA measurement in light and dark theme; colour-vision simulation (GDS, Orange).
10. Reduced motion: Remove animations setting respected; auto-play pausable (Accessible.org; Orange lists it under general settings) [reduced motion appears in fewer methodologies than the others].
11. Complete processes end to end, one per key task, not screen by screen (Appt-EM, GDS "at least one service end-to-end", ArcTouch "user journeys").
12. Screen-change behaviour: focus placement after navigation, dialogs, bottom sheets and tab switches (Accessiway, ScreenAudit "Structure and Grouping").

## 4. Auditor workflow and deliverables

### 4.1 Scoping

- Appt-EM (version 1.1.0, 4 July 2023, based on WCAG-EM) defines scope by download link, OS (each OS audited separately), version and installation method; records screens by navigation path; chooses conformance level (AA widely used); explores frequently used and essential screens, key flows and common UI elements; builds a structured sample plus a random sample of at least 10 percent of the structured sample; adds whole flows so complete processes are tested; requires screenshots; reports per WCAG-EM chapter 5 using app terminology and navigation paths instead of URLs (https://appt.org/en/guidelines/appt-em).
- Accessible.org: scope by screens and unique states (modals, error and empty states, permission prompts, post-action flows), 15 to 40 screens per platform, each platform audited separately with one report per platform, real devices (a recent iPhone on current iOS and a current Pixel or comparable Android), WCAG 2.1 AA default or 2.2 AA on request, native target minimums of 44 by 44 pt on iOS and 48 by 48 dp on Android, React Native and Flutter apps still audited per platform (https://accessible.org/conduct-audit-native-mobile-app/; https://accessible.org/android-ios-accessibility-audit/).
- GDS: 21 apps, each on Android and iOS (latest versions), Pixel 4a and iPhone 11, with TalkBack, VoiceOver, an external keyboard, Accessibility Scanner and the Colour Contrast Analyser, following the detailed website process across all WCAG 2.1 A and AA criteria; 13 to 76 issues per test, average 42 (GDS 2022 to 2024 report, URL above).
- ArcTouch and Fable: 50 apps in five industries scored per platform on four assistive technologies (screen reader, voice-based alternative navigation, font scaling, orientation) across key use cases forming user journeys; 85 and above Great, 30 to 49 Poor, 29 and below Failing (SOMAA PDF).
- Deque's generic scoping: key entry points, core paths, high-traffic pages and critical flows, with defined assistive-technology and platform combinations (https://www.deque.com/blog/what-to-look-for-in-an-accessibility-audit/, 9 September 2020).

Device matrix in practice: one current flagship per platform at minimum (GDS, Accessible.org), latest OS version, with font scale 200 percent and large display size as test states rather than separate devices; a small-screen or foldable device is recommended for reflow and the Android 16 sw600dp behaviour (inference from Android 16 docs, section 5).

### 4.2 Recording a finding

Fields in common across Deque, Accessible.org, Appt-EM and the Equalify GitHub issue form: screen or location (navigation path for apps), WCAG success criterion and level (plus EN clause where EN is the cited standard), description and user impact, severity, steps to reproduce, assistive technology and version, device and OS version and app build, screenshot or short recording, recommended fix with platform-specific code, and expected behaviour or expected screen-reader output (https://www.deque.com/blog/what-to-look-for-in-an-accessibility-audit/; https://accessible.org/conduct-audit-native-mobile-app/; https://appt.org/en/guidelines/appt-em; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EqualifyEverything/equalify-iris/main/.github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/accessibility.yml). The Equalify form is a reusable template: dropdown "Where did you find this?", Summary, Expected behavior, Actual behavior, Steps to reproduce (all required), Severity dropdown (Critical, High, Medium, Low), OS and version, Browser and version, Assistive technology and version (placeholder includes "TalkBack on Android 14"), Related WCAG criteria, Additional context; it applies an "accessibility" label and a "[a11y]:" title prefix. An older Markdown template asks "Which browser and/or Assistive Technology?" and step-by-step reproduction (https://gist.github.com/marcysutton/346b33739135d5eaa0337e6d31704288). No vendor-published Jira template was found; one guide argues the fields matter more than the tool and that a ticket is ready when someone who did not find the bug can reproduce it from the written steps (https://www.adacompliancepros.com/blog/accessibility-bug-report-template) [vendor].

### 4.4 ACR and VPAT 2.5 for software

ITI's VPAT 2.5Rev (April 2025, files dated 24 April 2025) comes in four editions: 508 (WCAG 2.0 incorporated), EU (EN 301 549 V3.2.1, WCAG 2.1 incorporated), WCAG (2.0, 2.1 and 2.2) and INT (all three). All editions contain a Product Description, Evaluation Methods Used and Notes fields, and version 2.5 added a column for a detailed explanation of each conformance level; "Partially Supports" replaced "Supports with Exceptions" at the US Access Board's request (https://www.itic.org/policy/accessibility/vpat). A completed VPAT is an Accessibility Conformance Report. Conformance terms as printed in the INT template: Supports, "The functionality of the product has at least one method that meets the criterion without known defects" or meets with equivalent facilitation; Partially Supports, "Some functionality of the product does not meet the criterion"; Does Not Support, "The majority of product functionality does not meet the criterion"; Not Applicable, "The criterion is not relevant to the product"; Not Evaluated, "The product has not been evaluated against the criterion", allowed only for WCAG AAA (https://docs.webchartnow.com/resources/system-specifications/vpat/vpat-2.5/vpat2.5rev_int_february2025/).

Chapters that apply to a native mobile app: WCAG tables 1 (A) and 2 (AA), optionally 3 (AAA); Revised Section 508 Chapter 3 Functional Performance Criteria (302.1 to 302.9), Chapter 5 Software (501.1, 502.2.x, 502.3.1 to 502.3.14, 502.4, 503.2, 503.3, 503.4.x, 504.x) and Chapter 6 Support Documentation (601 to 603); EN 301 549 clauses 4, 5, 11 (11.0 to 11.4 cross-referenced to WCAG, 11.5.2.x, 11.6.x, 11.7, and 11.8.x in the V3.2.1-based template) and 12, with 6, 7 and 13 only if the app provides voice, video or relay. Chapter 4 Hardware and EN clauses 8, 9 and 10 are normally Not Applicable for a native app. Each WCAG row lists the 508 and EN clauses it also answers (1.1.1 maps to 9.1.1.1, 10.1.1.1, 11.1.1.1.1, 11.1.1.1.2, 11.8.2, 12.1.2, 12.2.4 and to 501, 504.2, 602.3); rows marked "2.2 only" are "Does not apply" under 508 and EN V3.2.1; 4.1.1 is always Supports for 2.0 and 2.1 and does not apply to 2.2 (same template page). No VPAT edition for EN 301 549 V4.1.1 was found [unverified].

### 4.5 Accessibility statement requirements for apps (GOV.UK template)

Mandatory structure: scope statement (say "mobile application" instead of "website"), how accessible the app is, feedback and contact information, enforcement procedure with fixed wording and a link to EASS or ECNI, technical information with fixed wording and one compliance status (fully, partially or not compliant with WCAG 2.2 AA), non-accessible content in three required subsections (non-compliance with the regulations listing each issue with its WCAG criterion and fix date; disproportionate burden with a completed assessment; content not within scope), optional improvement plan, and preparation details (first published, last reviewed, last tested, testing method and who did it, reviewed at least yearly). For apps, include the version information and publication date and publish in the app store, on the website or both (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sample-accessibility-statement/sample-accessibility-statement-for-a-fictional-public-sector-website). GDS found 0 percent of app statements compliant at initial test, rising to 76 percent after monitoring (GDS report).

### 4.6 Tracking remediation

GDS gives bodies 12 weeks, requests a progress update, retests selectively, then recommends compliance action or refers to EHRC or ECNI (https://www.gov.uk/guidance/accessibility-monitoring-how-we-test). BBC treats P1 as release-blocking (BBC bugs guide). Deque's reports lead with an executive dashboard (conformance meter, issues by user impact, top issues by WCAG checkpoint) and counts by severity per page or component (Deque audit blog). Accessible.org includes a validation pass after fixes to confirm resolution and catch regressions (Accessible.org). Record retention is a legal point in Canada (four years for assessments, statements and training records) (canada.ca amendment summary) and India's draft rules require a signed, machine-readable ACR verified before deployment (Legal500 article).

## 5. Android developer guidance auditors check against

Platform principles (https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/apps; https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/views/principles-views; https://developer.android.com/design/ui/mobile/guides/foundations/accessibility):

- Touch targets: "each interactive UI element have a focusable area, or touch target size, of at least 48dpx48dp. Larger is even better"; "Ensure all touch targets are at least 48 dp, even if this extends past the UI element visual"; Compose Button, IconButton and ListItem enforce the minimum, custom elements need sizeIn(minWidth = 48.dp, minHeight = 48.dp); Google's support page allows padding to bring a 24 dp icon to a 48 dp target (https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858).
- Contrast: text under 18 sp, or bold under 14 sp, "at least 4.5:1"; other text "at least 3:1"; "Use a 3:1 ratio between surfaces and non-text elements"; body text "no smaller than 12 sp".
- Labels: contentDescription states purpose and result, not visual detail; use Role semantics for type, so "Submit" not "Submit button"; unique labels in lists (set in the adapter for RecyclerView); no contentDescription on Text composables; decorative images contentDescription = null or importantForAccessibility="no" or hideFromAccessibility; android:hint for example input and android:labelFor to associate a TextView label with an EditText; Compose uses label and placeholder.
- Grouping: container android:screenReaderFocusable="true" with children focusable="false" (API 19 and lower: container focusable), or Compose mergeDescendants = true; keep merged descriptions short and do not aggregate child text.
- Headings and panes: android:accessibilityHeading="true" or Compose heading(); android:accessibilityPaneTitle or Compose paneTitle (API 28 and later), unique per pane.
- Custom actions and gestures: ViewCompat.addAccessibilityAction and replaceAccessibilityAction (for example "Double tap and hold to favorite"); Compose customActions with CustomAccessibilityAction for swipe or drag flows so TalkBack, Voice Access and Switch Access users can complete them; onClickLabel and onLongClickLabel.
- State and value: stateDescription, toggleableState, Role; progressBarRangeInfo; collectionInfo and collectionItemInfo; error(); liveRegion Polite in most cases, Assertive sparingly, never on timers (https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/accessibility/semantics).
- Traversal: Compose isTraversalGroup on containers and traversalIndex (float, lower first, default 0, negatives allowed) on focusable children (https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/accessibility/traversal); View equivalents accessibilityTraversalBefore and After [the View API names are from general knowledge, the Compose page does not list them].
- Colour not sole cue: "Use more than one visual affordance for actions like links"; haptic feedback as an additional channel.
- Material Design 3: m3.material.io/foundations/accessible-design pages render client-side and returned no text (overview) or 404 (accessibility-basics) in this session, so the Material-based figures above are taken from the Android design foundations page, which is Material-derived [M3 pages unverified].

Text scaling and Android 14 (https://developer.android.com/about/versions/14/features): font scaling up to 200 percent with a non-linear curve so large text scales less than small text; test with Font size at maximum; always use sp for text and lineHeight (TextView corrects lineHeight only when both are sp); do not use sp for padding or heights ("4sp + 20sp might not equal 24sp"); use TypedValue.applyDimension() and deriveDimension(); DisplayMetrics.scaledDensity "is no longer accurate" and Configuration.fontScale is informational only.

Android 16 (https://developer.android.com/about/versions/16/features; https://developer.android.com/about/versions/16/behavior-changes-all; https://developer.android.com/about/versions/16/behavior-changes-16):

- Outline text "replacing high contrast text, which draws a larger contrasting area around text"; AccessibilityManager.isHighContrastTextEnabled() and a state-change listener for custom text rendering. An auditor should test the app with Outline text on.
- announceForAccessibility and TYPE_ANNOUNCEMENT are deprecated because they "create inconsistent user experiences for users of TalkBack"; use setAccessibilityPaneTitle or Activity.setTitle (Compose paneTitle) for window changes, setAccessibilityLiveRegion (Compose liveRegion) sparingly for critical changes, and CONTENT_CHANGE_TYPE_ERROR with AccessibilityNodeInfo.setError or TextView.setError for errors.
- New semantics: setSupplementalDescription for ViewGroups, setFieldRequired for form fields (3.3.2), setExpandedState with CONTENT_CHANGE_TYPE_EXPANDED (4.1.2 state), tri-state setChecked(int), RANGE_TYPE_INDETERMINATE for progress bars, list-based addLabeledBy, TtsSpan TYPE_DURATION.
- LE Audio hearing aids: phone-microphone switching for calls and ambient volume control (user-facing).
- Large screens: for apps targeting API 36, "orientation, resizability, and aspect ratio restrictions no longer apply on displays with smallest width >= 600dp"; screenOrientation, resizeableActivity, minAspectRatio, maxAspectRatio and setRequestedOrientation are ignored; temporary opt-out via PROPERTY_COMPAT_ALLOW_RESTRICTED_RESIZABILITY, which "won't apply when targeting API level 37"; games exempt. For 1.3.4 this means orientation locks are already moot on tablets and unfolded foldables, and auditors should test both orientations on phones where locks still apply.

Testing tools auditors expect developers to have run (https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/testing): Accessibility Scanner, Play Console pre-launch report, Compose UI Check (contrast, text stretched on large screens, colour-vision simulation), Espresso with AccessibilityChecks, UI Automator Viewer for the semantics tree, and manual TalkBack and Switch Access passes with the documented checks (every element reachable, succinct announcements, alerts read, switch highlights only actionable items once).

## 6. Common failure patterns in real Android apps and published prevalence

### 6.1 Regulator data: GDS monitoring of 21 public-sector apps (2022 to 2024)

Share of all 880 app issues by criterion: 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value 19.2 percent; 1.3.1 Info and Relationships 14.7 percent; 1.4.3 Contrast 13.6 percent; 2.1.1 Keyboard 10.6 percent; 1.4.4 Resize Text 6.0 percent; 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast 5.8 percent; 2.4.6 Headings and Labels 5.5 percent; 1.1.1 Non-text Content 4.1 percent; 2.4.7 Focus Visible 3.9 percent; 4.1.3 Status Messages 2.4 percent (top five 64 percent, top ten 86 percent). Recurring problems: large sections not keyboard accessible, content not scaling with system text settings, no support for both orientations; keyboard and orientation issues were least likely to be fixed; 84 percent of issues resolved during monitoring; 57 percent of app cases claimed disproportionate burden; no app was fully compliant at first test (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/accessibility-monitoring-of-public-sector-websites-and-mobile-apps-from-2022-to-2024/accessibility-monitoring-of-public-sector-websites-and-mobile-apps-from-2022-to-2024).

### 6.2 Industry study: ArcTouch and Fable, State of Mobile App Accessibility (2025)

50 apps, five industries, four assistive technologies: 72 percent of user journeys contained barriers that produced a Poor or Failing experience; Android apps averaged an App Accessibility Score of 56 against 48 for iOS; two apps Great, nine Failing; by assistive technology the share of journeys with a Poor or Failing screen was 62 percent for screen reader, 68 percent for alternative navigation (voice control) and 64 percent for font scaling; in Shopping it reached 85 percent. Shopping specifics: all 10 apps lacked support for both orientations; 76.7 percent of screens had name, role and value issues; 58.3 percent of screens had focus order issues; 43.4 percent of screens lacked full font scaling support, and 35 percent of those with some support lost information when scaled; 48 percent of home-screen attributes scored Poor or Failing. The report's conclusion is that "native accessibility tools alone are not enough" and that builders are "focused on screens, not user journeys" (SOMAA PDF, https://assets.ctfassets.net/efnbmai9c55m/28ix8rGHfvVhl8jdcEBj4Q/99dc81521ef3422ac3aeda933ed10407/ArcTouch_SOMAA_Report.pdf; https://arctouch.com/state-of-mobile-app-accessibility).

### 6.3 Academic large-scale studies

- Alshayban, Ahmed and Malek, ICSE 2020, 1,000-plus Android apps checked with the Accessibility Test Framework: mean inaccessibility rate per app 6.04 percent of elements; by type (mean percent of elements failing): TextContrast 22.81, TouchTargetSize 19.78, ImageContrast 12.85, SpeakableText 11.08, RedundantDescription 0.93, DuplicateSpeakableText 0.89, ClassName 0.68, DuplicateClickableBounds 0.55, EditableContentDesc 0.31, ClickableSpan 0.15, TraversalOrder 0.0; the top developer-reported cause was "Lack of awareness about accessibility and its importance" (48.53 percent), then additional cost (16.50) and lack of management support (15.53) (https://seal.ics.uci.edu/publications/2019_ICSE_Accessibility.pdf).
- Xbot (2022), 2,270 apps (1,172 Google Play, 1,098 F-Droid): 88.99 percent of apps had at least one issue; 86,767 issues, 43 per app on average; item label, item descriptions, touch target, text contrast and image contrast were 93.1 percent of issues, with touch target, text contrast and item label each over 20,000; TextView, ImageView, Button, EditText and ImageButton accounted for 93.1 percent of issues; touch-target widths in F-Droid apps clustered around 20 dp; across 70 apps and 210 versions, 81.43 percent showed no change in issue counts and the paper states 96 percent of issues remained unfixed (https://arxiv.org/html/2203.06422v1).
- Fok, Zhong, Ross, Fogarty and Wobbrock, CHI 2022, 312 apps crawled monthly for 16 months (3,775 crawls): missing-label failures affected 55.6 percent of unique image-based elements; 8.8 percent of unique screens could not be reached without passing a missing-label failure; ImageButton labelling improved over time but the more common ImageView did not; failure rates did not improve with download counts (https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-22.02.pdf; https://www.github.com/appaccess/LAMA-CHI2022).
- ScreenAudit, CHI 2025: expert ground truth on 14 screens found Structure and Grouping (54), Label Quality (42) and Missing Label (39) the most frequent screen-reader error categories; Scanner coverage 31.3 percent (https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-25.02.pdf).
- Indika, Kazman and Peruma, ICSE 2026, survey of 110 mobile developers in 43 countries: 35.11 percent knew the Android Accessibility Guidelines, 32.98 percent the iOS guidelines, 10.11 percent WCAG, 7.98 percent the W3C mobile guidance and 8.51 percent none; the most implemented features were resizable text (11.67 percent of responses), text-to-speech (10.77) and screen reader compatibility (9.52); 43.14 percent of testing responses were manual testing, 22.55 percent automated and 13.24 percent used assistive technologies; compliance testing typically happens late, during QA and pre-release (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.14131).

### 6.4 Vendor audit data (treat as indicative)

Accessiway, 50 iOS and Android apps audited to WCAG 2.2 AA with 6,652 issues (published 23 October 2026): share of apps affected, 4.1.2 96 percent, 1.3.1 96 percent, 2.4.3 96 percent, 4.1.3 94 percent, 1.1.1 94 percent, 2.1.1 92 percent, 1.4.4 92 percent; named failures: missing or wrong name, role or state 96 percent, element unreachable by screen reader 84 percent, bottom navigation not moving focus 72 percent, headings not marked 62 percent (https://www.accessiway.com/blog/mobile-app-accessibility-errors) [vendor, methodology not independently verified].

### 6.5 Why it matters to users: assistive-technology usage

Appt's survey of about five million Dutch iOS and Android users (updated 4 December 2025) reports that exactly half of iOS users and almost three quarters of Android users have one or more accessibility settings activated (https://appt.org/en/stats). Fable's community in the ArcTouch study used iOS weekly at 74 percent against 43 percent for Android, yet Android apps scored higher (SOMAA PDF).

### 6.6 Prioritisation for an on-device rule set

Combining the regulator, journey-based, automated and longitudinal datasets above, the prevalence ordering for Android is:

1. Name, role, value and label quality on controls (4.1.2, 1.1.1, 2.4.6): top of every manual dataset (GDS 19.2 percent plus 4.1 percent plus 5.5 percent; ArcTouch 76.7 percent of shopping screens; Accessiway 96 percent of apps; Fok 55.6 percent of image elements unlabelled). Automation finds the missing-label subset only; label quality, role and state need TalkBack.
2. Structure and relationships, headings, focus order and focus management after navigation (1.3.1, 2.4.3): GDS 14.7 percent; ScreenAudit's largest category; Accessiway 96 percent and 72 percent (bottom navigation). Almost entirely manual.
3. Text contrast (1.4.3) and non-text contrast (1.4.11): GDS 13.6 plus 5.8 percent; the largest automated category in Alshayban (22.81 percent of elements) and Xbot. Highly automatable, but must be re-run in dark theme and at 200 percent.
4. Keyboard operability (2.1.1, 2.4.7): GDS 10.6 plus 3.9 percent and least likely to be fixed; absent from automated datasets because ATF does not test it.
5. Text scaling and reflow (1.4.4, 1.4.10): GDS 6.0 percent; ArcTouch 64 percent of journeys and 43.4 percent of shopping screens; Accessiway 92 percent of apps. Semi-automatable (TextSizeCheck for fixed dp) but layout breakage needs a person at 200 percent.
6. Touch target size (2.5.8): second largest automated category (Alshayban 19.78 percent of elements, Xbot over 20,000) but low in regulator data because 2.5.8 is new in WCAG 2.2 and GDS audited to 2.1. Fully automatable in dp.
7. Orientation (1.3.4): all 10 ArcTouch shopping apps failed; GDS recurring and rarely fixed. Trivially testable by rotation, partially inferable from manifest, and changing with Android 16 on large screens.
8. Status messages (4.1.3): GDS 2.4 percent, Accessiway 94 percent of apps; manual with TalkBack, and the Android 16 deprecation of announceForAccessibility will shift implementations toward live regions.
9. Gesture alternatives and voice navigation (2.5.1, 2.5.7, 2.5.3): ArcTouch alternative navigation 68 percent of journeys Poor or Failing; not covered by regulator data or automation.
